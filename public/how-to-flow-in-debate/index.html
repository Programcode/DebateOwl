How to Flow in Debate

Flowing is the skill that separates good debaters from great ones.

Published July 5, 2026 · Updated October 28, 2026

By the DebateOwl team

Start practicingSee DebateOwl

How do you flow a debate round?

Take notes in columns, one per speech in speaking order, with a separate sheet or section for each side's case. Write each argument's tag in a few words with the reason it is true underneath, use two colours, and leave space. A short, consistent set of abbreviations lets you keep up. Mark any argument with nothing next to it in the next column as dropped, since most formats treat it as conceded. Paper and a laptop both work. In British Parliamentary and World Schools, many debaters flow by clash instead of strictly by speech.

Column structure

One column per speech, so each argument can be followed across the round from where it starts to where it is answered or dropped.

Abbreviations that keep up

A short, consistent set of symbols, so you can write as fast as the round moves.

Dropped argument tracking

A habit of marking what was not answered, because a dropped argument is usually a conceded one.

Why flowing matters

Flowing is taking notes in a structure that follows arguments through the round. If you cannot see the structure of the debate, you cannot respond strategically: you answer what you remember, not what matters, and you miss what the other side dropped.

Judges flow too. When your speech follows the same structure as their flow, the judge can see each response land on the argument it answers.

How to set up your flow

  1. Use one column per speech, left to right in speaking order. In Lincoln-Douglas that is the affirmative constructive, the negative constructive, then each rebuttal.
  2. Keep a separate sheet, or section, for each side's case. Responses to the affirmative go on the affirmative sheet next to the argument they answer.
  3. Write each argument's short label, with the reason it is true underneath (debaters call this the warrant). The label helps you find the argument; the reason is what you need to test.
  4. Use two colours, one for each side, so you can see at a glance who said what.
  5. Leave space. Arguments grow across speeches, and a cramped flow is the one that loses track.

To skip ruling the columns by hand, print a free debate flow sheet with one column per speech for your format.

Abbreviations

You cannot write full sentences at speaking speed. Keep a short list and use it every time, so you can read your own flow under pressure:

  • Arrows for causes and leads to, and a crossed arrow for does not lead to.
  • Up and down arrows for increase and decrease.
  • Short forms for common words: gov for government, econ for economy, ev for evidence.
  • A circled letter or number for each argument, so responses can point to it.
  • A question mark for a claim the speaker gave no reason for.

Tracking dropped arguments

When an argument has nothing written next to it in the next column, it was dropped. Mark it straight away. In most formats a dropped argument is treated as conceded, and pointing that out is often the shortest path to winning a clash.

Check your own side too. Before each speech, look along your columns for anything of yours the other side answered that you have not yet responded to. Those are the gaps a good opponent will extend.

Use your flow to write speeches

Before you speak, look at the flow and decide which clashes you must win. Then speak down the flow in order, so the judge can follow each response to its argument. Your speech should trace the flow, not introduce random new material.

In British Parliamentary and World Schools, with more speakers and no fixed pairing of speeches, many debaters flow by clash rather than strictly by speech: a section for each main question in the debate, with every team's points on it.

How to get better at flowing

Flow recorded rounds and compare your flow with the speech afterwards to see what you missed. Start with slower speeches and build up. After a practice round, check whether anything you dropped was dropped by choice or by accident. Frequently asked questions

What does flowing mean in debate?

Taking notes on a round in columns, one per speech, so each argument can be followed from where it is made to where it is answered or dropped.

Should I flow on paper or a laptop?

Either works. Paper is easier for drawing arrows and links; a laptop is easier to keep tidy. Use whichever you can write on quickly and read under pressure.

What is a dropped argument?

An argument the other side never answered. In most formats a dropped argument is treated as conceded, so mark it on your flow and point it out.

How do I flow faster?

Use a consistent set of abbreviations, write short labels and reasons rather than full sentences, and practise on recorded rounds before tournaments.
