Debate Judge Adaptation Guide

Read the judge, adapt your style, and frame the ballot they want to write.

How do you adapt your debating to different types of judges?

Find out what the judge prefers before the round, from a published paradigm or by asking. Lay judges reward a clear story, plain language and a few arguments at a normal pace. Flow judges reward line-by-line answers, named extensions and explicit weighing. Technical judges add evidence quality and strategy. Change how many arguments you run and how you present them, not the claims you defend, and watch the judge's note-taking to adjust during the round.

What judges have in common

Every judge has to write a reason for their decision. Adapting means making that reason easy to write: a clear comparison they can repeat, in language they understand, at a pace they can follow. The style changes from judge to judge; the job does not.

Three kinds of judges

JudgeWhat they rewardHow to adapt
Lay judge (parent, teacher, community member)A clear story, plain language, confident deliveryFewer arguments, no jargon, a normal speaking pace and a one-sentence reason to vote
Flow judge (former debater or coach)Line-by-line clash, extensions, explicit weighingSignpost every response, extend warrants by name, never drop a turned argument
Technical judge (experienced circuit judge)Evidence quality, strategic choices, theory and frameworkPrecise extensions, efficient speed if they allow it, clear interpretations on theory

Most judges sit somewhere between these. A former debater who now judges local tournaments may flow carefully but dislike speed, so read the individual, not the category.

Before the round

Many tournaments publish judge paradigms, short statements of what a judge prefers. Read them during the pairing announcement. If there is no paradigm, ask politely before the round starts: "Do you have any preferences on speed or on how you weigh?" Most judges are glad to be asked.

In British Parliamentary and other university formats, judges are asked to judge as the ordinary intelligent voter. The WUDC Debating & Judging Manual describes that voter as someone who reads the front pages and world section of a major newspaper but no specialist literature. In practice that means explaining examples rather than just citing them, whoever the judge is.

Reading the room during the round

Watch the judge while you speak. A judge who stops writing during a technical section has probably stopped following it; slow down and restate the point in plainer words. A judge who nods at a comparison is telling you which argument is landing, so spend your remaining time there.

Adapt your cross-examination as well. Lay judges watch questioning as a contest of composure, so stay calm and brief. Flow judges listen for concessions, so make each one explicit and repeat it in your next speech.

Worked example: one argument, two judges

Practice motion: This House would make public libraries open 24 hours in large cities

The argument: Night-shift workers cannot use libraries during standard hours, so 24-hour opening reaches people current hours exclude.

For a lay judge: "Think of a nurse who works nights. Today the library is closed every hour she is free. Opening all night is the only way she gets the same library everyone else has." One example, one clear impact, said slowly.

For a flow judge: "Extend our first contention: access for shift workers. Their only answer was cost, but they never contested that current hours exclude this group. Weigh it: exclusion is certain and ongoing; their cost harm depends on staffing numbers they never gave."

What stays the same: The warrant (current hours exclude a group) and the comparison against cost. Only the framing and pace change.

Neither version is better in general. The lay version would lose a careful flow judge, who wants to hear which response was dropped, and the flow version would lose a lay judge in the first ten seconds.

Common mistakes

  • Treating adaptation as only changing speed, when the bigger change is how many arguments you run.
  • Using jargon such as "extend", "turn" or "link" with a lay judge without explaining what it means.
  • Giving a technical judge a story without line-by-line answers, so dropped arguments go uncounted.
  • Deciding a judge is lay from their appearance instead of reading their paradigm or asking.
  • Changing your arguments for the judge. Adapt the presentation and selection, not the claims you are willing to defend.

A 15-minute adaptation drill

  1. Two minutes: take one argument from your current case and write its warrant and impact in two sentences.
  2. Four minutes: rewrite it as a lay-judge version with one concrete example and no debate vocabulary.
  3. Four minutes: rewrite it as a flow-judge version with signposts, an extension and an explicit weighing line.
  4. Four minutes: deliver both, timed at one minute each, and record them.
  5. One minute: play both back and check that the warrant is the same in each.

Frequently asked questions

What is a judge paradigm?

A short statement, often published by the tournament, of what a judge prefers: speed, evidence, theory, and how they weigh arguments.

How do I know what kind of judge I have?

Read their paradigm if there is one, ask before the round, and watch how they take notes once you start speaking.

Should I speak faster for an experienced judge?

Only if their paradigm or answer says they are comfortable with it. Many experienced judges prefer a moderate pace with clear signposting.

What is the ordinary intelligent voter?

The standard BP and many university judges are asked to use: a well-informed reader of the news without specialist knowledge, who needs examples explained.
