How do I use AI to prepare for a debate?

Use DebateOwl for the slow parts you would rather not invent from a blank page: read the motion, draft two to four arguments with evidence, list what the other side will say and write an "If they say… we answer…" reply to each, then practise at real speech times. Keep the judgement calls for yourself, and check every source before it goes into a speech. A general chatbot can do pieces of this if you prompt it.

A worked second speech: phones in class

Motion: "This House would ban phones in class." The same motion appears on the DebateOwl homepage. Nothing below is a real study. The brackets are placeholders you replace after you have opened a source.

Opening line. Today we will show two arguments that prove phones belong outside class.

Argument 1, in our team's first speech

Notifications break concentration. Explanation: a student who looks at a message then spends the next explanation catching up has missed the step the rest of the room is on. Evidence: [a study on refocus time: name the source and year you verified]. Why it matters: a lesson only works if the room is in it together.

Argument 2, so the second speech has two points to back up

Class is a shared conversation. Explanation: when some students are on their phones, the people speaking lose the listeners the lesson depends on. Evidence: [a classroom observation you have checked: name the source and year]. Why it matters: a discussion you cannot join is not a discussion.

Second speech, about 6 minutes

Part 1, about 2 minutes, reinforce. No new arguments. Back up the first argument with one fresh example: the student who glances down and then asks a question the room already answered. Back up the second with one fresh example: a partner task where one person is scrolling and the other is talking to an empty chair.

Part 2, about 4 minutes, "If they say X, we answer Y".

  1. If they say "Students need phones for emergencies", we answer: the school office can call home, and a phone in a bag at the office covers the same emergency without a screen on every desk.
  2. If they say "Phones are learning tools", we answer: when a task actually needs a device, the class can share a computer or a set of tablets, which is different from every student having a private phone out all period.
  3. If they say "A ban is unenforceable", we answer: schools already collect items that distract, and a basket at the door is easier to see than a rule about using a phone well.
  4. If they say "Students must learn self-control", we answer: self-control is practised when the distraction is not buzzing during the explanation. The lesson is the place to pay attention.
  5. If they say "Parents want to reach their children", we answer: parents can call the office, and students can check messages at lunch. A class period is short enough that a delay is not the same as being unreachable.

Closing line. That is why phones belong outside the lesson: the class stays on the work, and a real emergency still has a way through.

What you still decide yourself

The plan above will hand you a full draft. The round is still yours. Three decisions should not be delegated, even when the draft sounds confident.

The first is the motion's meaning. If "ban" in your motion means a legal prohibition and the draft treats it as a classroom norm, the whole case is about the wrong world. Read the actor, the action and the word that sets the strength of the claim before you fall in love with an argument. Brainstorm will offer readings. You pick the one a judge would accept without a fight.

The second is which argument you are willing to live on. Two to four arguments is the shape. Three of them can be true and only one of them can be the thing you return to in the last speech. If you cannot say, in one sentence, why that argument matters more than the other side's best point, you do not yet have a case. You have a list.

The third is the source. A named year is not a check. Open the page, find the sentence, and see whether it is about your claim or about a neighbouring one. If you cannot find it, the sentence leaves the speech. This is the same rule for DebateOwl, for ChatGPT and for Claude. Active Grounding, on the paid plan, is a research aid. It is not a substitute for the tab you open yourself.

A practical test before you close the laptop: give the second speech to an empty room, at about six minutes, with a timer. If part 1 starts a new argument, stop and cut it. If part 2 answers a point the other side would never say, replace that block with one they would. If you run out of time before the closing line, the reinforcement was too long. The free timer will do this drill for any format. Sparring will do it against an opponent for School 4-6-6-3, Lincoln-Douglas and Public Forum, and it will tell you a reason at the end. Change one thing from that reason. Then stop.

Teachers who want the class to do this without accounts can put the timer on a projector and print the flow sheet. If you only have twenty minutes, do the smallest version. Read the motion, generate the case, say the second speech's reply blocks once, and open one source. That is a complete prep, not a sketch you feel guilty about. The rest of the week can add the timed round.

Questions about using AI for prep

Can AI help me prepare for a debate?

Yes, as long as you treat what it gives you as a draft. Use it for the slow parts, and keep the judgement calls. Check every source before it goes into a speech.

What should a second speech include?

No new arguments: about two minutes backing up your team's arguments, then about four minutes of "If they say X, we answer Y" replies.

Is using AI for debate prep cheating?

Prep is usually fine, but most leagues restrict electronics or AI during the round itself. Check your league's rules.

How do I stop the AI making up evidence?

Ask for the source and year of every fact, open each one, and cut anything you can't find.
