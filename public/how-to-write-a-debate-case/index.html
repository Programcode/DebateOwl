How to Write a Debate Case

Move from motion analysis to an opening, arguments with evidence, answers to the other side, and a closing line.

Published July 5, 2026 · Updated October 29, 2026

By the DebateOwl team

How do you write a debate case?

A DebateOwl case has an opening, two to four arguments (three by default) each with an explanation, evidence and why it matters, "If they say… we answer…" replies, a closing line and the sources it used. Start by defining the motion fairly and writing what your side must prove. Check every source, rewrite the draft in your own words and time a reading aloud. Adjust for your format: Public Forum usually fits two arguments; Lincoln-Douglas uses a goal and a test for meeting it; BP closing teams need a new angle.

Motion analysis first

Define the terms, write down what your side has to prove and find where the two sides disagree before writing a single argument.

Arguments that hold up

Build each argument as a point, an explanation, evidence and why it matters, so a judge can follow it and an opponent cannot break it with one question.

A case you can defend

Write "If they say… we answer…" replies to the obvious responses, cut what does not clash, and test the draft by attacking it yourself.

What a debate case is

A debate case is the argument your side will defend for the whole round, organised so a judge can follow it on first hearing. It is not an essay. An essay can explore; a case has to survive an opponent whose job is to find the weakest sentence in it. That is why good cases are built from small, testable parts: an opening that tells the judge what you will prove, two to four arguments that each prove something specific, answers to what the other side will say, and a closing line the judge remembers.

Every case DebateOwl writes follows that shape, so you can hold a generated draft next to your own and compare them part by part. The process below works for every format. The format changes the length, the number of arguments and who gives which part, which the format section covers further down.

Eight steps from motion to finished case

  1. Read the motion word by word. Note the actor (who does it), the action (what changes) and any word that sets the strength of the claim, such as should, would, ban, regulate or prioritise. A motion about regulating something is a different debate from one about banning it.
  2. Define the key terms in plain language. Pick definitions that are fair and that a judge would accept without argument. A squirrelled definition wins the definition debate and loses the round.
  3. Write down in one sentence what your side has to prove for the judge to vote for you. If you are proposing a policy, you usually need to show a problem, a mechanism and a net benefit. If you are opposing, you choose whether to defend the status quo, a counter-proposal or simply that the proposal does more harm than good.
  4. Brainstorm widely, then cut hard. List every argument for your side, group the ones that share a reason, and keep the two to four groups that are strongest and most different from each other.
  5. Build each argument in four parts: the point (what is true, in one sentence), the explanation (why it is true, step by step), the evidence (a source that backs it, named with its year) and why it matters (what it means for real people, and why that matters more than what the other side will say).
  6. Write the opening: a hook that makes the judge listen, then one line that says what you will show, such as "Today we will show two arguments that prove…". In Lincoln-Douglas, your value and criterion go here too.
  7. For each argument, write down what the other side is most likely to say and a short answer: "If they say…, we answer…". Put the answer to the most obvious response inside the argument itself, so the opponent's best attack does not sound new.
  8. Finish with a closing line that ties back to the opening and a list of your sources. Then read the case aloud against a timer and cut until it fits with fifteen seconds to spare.

Worked example: a full case outline

Motion: This house would make public transport free

Side: Proposition.

Definitions: Public transport means buses, trams and local trains run or funded by government. Free means no fare at the point of use, paid for from general taxation.

What we have to prove: That removing fares produces benefits greater than the cost of replacing fare revenue.

Opening: For a family on a low income, the bus fare to work can be the difference between taking a job and turning it down. Today we will show two arguments that prove free public transport is worth what it costs.

Argument 1, access: Point: fares stop low-income people from reaching jobs and services. Explanation: transport is a fixed cost that takes a larger share of a small income. Why it matters: removing it widens the area in which people can work and study. Answer built in: discounted passes exist, but they require applications and proof of eligibility that many eligible people never complete.

Argument 2, congestion and emissions: Point: free fares move some journeys from cars to public transport. Explanation: price is one of the things people consider when choosing how to travel. Why it matters: fewer car journeys reduce congestion and emissions for everyone in the city.

Order: Argument 1 is the more certain benefit and reaches the people most affected, so it goes first; argument 2 is larger in scale but less certain, so it is an additional reason, not the case's foundation.

If they say: The money is better spent on more frequent services.

We answer: Frequency helps people who already ride; free fares reach people currently priced out, and the two are not mutually exclusive.

Closing line: That is why free fares are the fairest way to pay for the buses a whole city depends on.

Notice what the outline does not contain: a long introduction, rhetorical questions or a list of statistics without explanations. Every line either defines the debate, proves something, answers the other side or tells the judge what to remember.

How case structure changes by format

Public Forum (PF)

A four-minute constructive usually fits two arguments, each with evidence, and sometimes a short explanation of how the judge should decide. Because you often do not know your side until shortly before the round, write a case for each side and keep them consistent in style. Put answers to the other side into the second constructive if you speak second.

Lincoln-Douglas (LD)

The case starts with a value, the goal the judge should protect, and a criterion, the test for meeting that goal. Each argument then shows how your side passes the test. State that link at the end of every argument, because a good negative will attack any gap.

British Parliamentary (BP)

Opening Government sets the definition and, for policy motions, a model: what exactly happens, who does it and how it is enforced. Opening Opposition responds to that model. Closing teams do not write a full case of their own; they prepare an extension, a new line of analysis the opening half did not cover.

World Schools (WSDC)

The case is split between the first and second speakers under a single team line. The first speaker gives definitions, the burden and usually the first argument; the second speaker gives the remaining arguments. Write the split before you write the arguments, so neither speaker repeats the other.

Checklist before you call the case finished

  • Can you state the whole case in two sentences?
  • Does every argument have an explanation you could give without your notes?
  • Is every piece of evidence one you have read in the original, with a full citation?
  • Does each argument say why it matters, and why that matters more than what the other side will argue?
  • Have you written an "If they say…" answer to the strongest response to each argument?
  • Does it fit in the time with room to slow down?

If any answer is no, fix that before you practise delivery. Polishing the speech of a case with a missing explanation only makes the gap harder to see.

Frequently asked questions

How many contentions should a debate case have?

Usually two to four arguments. Public Forum constructives often fit two; formats with longer speeches can take three or four. Fewer well-defended arguments beat many thin ones.

What goes into each argument?

Say what is true, explain why it is true, give evidence you have checked and show why it matters. Add a short answer to the response you expect from the other side.

How long does it take to write a debate case?

A first draft can take an hour once you have researched the topic. Expect to revise it several times after practice rounds expose its weak points.

Should I write my case word for word?

In formats with prepared constructives, such as Public Forum and Lincoln-Douglas, most debaters do. In impromptu formats like BP and WSDC, prepare an outline and key lines instead.

How do I make a case harder to attack?

Explain why every point is true, use evidence you have checked, answer the most obvious response inside each argument, and attack the draft yourself before the round.
