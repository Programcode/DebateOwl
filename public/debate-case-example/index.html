How these cases were made

  • Each case was made with DebateOwl's Generate tool, the way a student uses it: start a debate with the motion and a format setting, generate the first speech (R1), then generate the second speech (R2) in the same debate, so the R2 can see and back up the R1.
  • Model: Claude Sonnet 5.5 (anthropic/claude-sonnet-5.5), the default model for Pro accounts. Length Standard, audience Intermediate and tone Formal, which are the form's defaults; reasoning effort low.
  • Web research ran live for every speech. A case can only link a page that its own research returned; DebateOwl removes any other link before the case is shown.
  • The runs used a local copy of DebateOwl rather than debateowl.com, so no student account or data was involved. One step differs from the live app: the short step that writes the web search queries also ran on Claude Sonnet 5.5 here, where the app uses Claude Haiku 5.5.
  • One attempt failed: the first Hungarian R2 stopped with an error, so the Hungarian sample was generated again from the start. No other output was rerun or picked over another.

For cases, DebateOwl currently supports the British Parliamentary, Policy and Classroom format settings. Whatever the setting, it writes each speech for a four-speaker team in the 4-6-6-3 structure: Introductor (4 minutes), R1 (6 minutes), R2 (6 minutes) and Conclusor (3 minutes). Opening

At 16 you can work, pay tax and serve in the armed forces, yet you cannot vote on the laws that govern all three. Today we say that gap has no justification.

What we will show: Today we will show three arguments that prove lowering the voting age to 16 is fair, builds lasting voting habits, and strengthens democracy.

Arguments

  1. Argument 1: Rights follow responsibilities

    Point: Sixteen-year-olds already carry adult duties, so they should have a say in the rules that bind them.

    Explanation: At 16 in the UK, young people can work full time, pay income tax and national insurance, and join the armed forces. Each of these makes them subject to decisions made by government.     Why it matters: Young workers and taxpayers are governed by laws they cannot influence. Giving them a vote ends that unfairness.

  2. Argument 2: Voting habits start earlier

    Point: Voting at 16 builds a lifelong habit of turning out, because first votes are easier at home and in school.

    Explanation: At 18, many people leave home or move to university. They move often, struggle to register, and do not know where to vote. At 16, young people live with family and are in education, so they get support through their first vote. People who vote early are more likely to keep voting, so a better first experience carries into adult life.

    Evidence: The Electoral Reform Society said in 2026 that in Austria, Scotland and Germany, those enfranchised at 16 or 17 were more likely to vote into their twenties than those who first voted at 18. It also noted registration for 18- and 19-year-olds is just 60%, against 96% for those aged 65 and over.

    Why it matters: Habits formed in a supported setting make democracy healthier for decades, not just at the next election.

  3. Argument 3: Closing the youth turnout gap

    Point: Lowering the voting age tackles a widening gap in turnout between young and older voters.

    Explanation: Young people are being left behind in politics. When they vote less, politicians have less reason to listen to them. Bringing 16- and 17-year-olds in early, while schools can teach them about politics, gives them a way into the system before the habit of staying out sets in.

Frequently asked questions

Can AI write a debate case?

Yes, as a draft. An AI case generator such as DebateOwl takes a motion, a side and a speaker role and writes the speech: an opening, arguments with evidence and why they matter, answers to what the other side is likely to say, a closing line and a source list. The samples on this page are unedited examples. A debater still has to open every source, check that it says what the case claims, and put the case into their own words before a round.

What should an AI debate case include?

The parts a debater prepares by hand: an opening with a hook and a line saying what you will prove; two to four arguments, each with a point, an explanation, evidence that names its source and year, and why it matters; “If they say… we answer…” replies to the other side's likely claims; a closing line; and a list of sources with links you can open. A second speech should back up the team's arguments and answer the other side instead of adding new arguments.
